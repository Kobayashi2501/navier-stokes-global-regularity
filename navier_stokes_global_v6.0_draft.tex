\section{Chapter 1: Introduction and Problem Setup}

\subsection{1.1 The Navier--Stokes Equations}

The three-dimensional incompressible Navier--Stokes equations describe the motion of viscous fluid flows and are given by:
\[
\partial_t u + (u \cdot \nabla)u = -\nabla p + \nu \Delta u, \quad \nabla \cdot u = 0,
\]
where \( u(t,x) \in \mathbb{R}^3 \) is the velocity field, \( p(t,x) \) is the pressure, and \( \nu > 0 \) is the kinematic viscosity. These equations model a wide class of physical phenomena including air, water, and other classical fluids.

Despite their apparent simplicity, the nonlinear convective term \( (u \cdot \nabla)u \) creates profound mathematical challenges, particularly regarding the global-in-time behavior of solutions.

\subsection{1.2 The Millennium Problem: Global Regularity}

The Clay Mathematics Institute has identified the following fundamental open problem as one of the Millennium Prize Problems:

\begin{quote}
\textit{Given smooth initial data with finite energy, does there exist a unique, globally smooth solution \( u(t,x) \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R}^3 \times [0,\infty)) \) to the 3D incompressible Navier--Stokes equations, or can singularities develop in finite time?}
\end{quote}

This question remains unresolved despite substantial analytical progress. Known results confirm existence of global weak solutions (Leray–Hopf solutions), but their regularity and uniqueness remain elusive in three dimensions.

\subsection{1.3 Previous Approaches and Limitations}

Many analytic techniques have been developed to address this problem, including:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Ladyzhenskaya–Prodi–Serrin criteria} based on integrability bounds of the velocity field,
  \item \textbf{Beale–Kato–Majda (BKM) criterion} linking blow-up to the growth of vorticity,
  \item \textbf{Energy methods} focusing on bounding the \( H^1 \) norm of solutions,
  \item \textbf{Geometric and spectral approaches} examining scale-invariance and energy cascades,
  \item \textbf{Numerical simulations} to trace singularity formation and vortex stretching.
\end{itemize}

However, no existing framework has succeeded in proving or disproving global regularity from a structurally complete, obstruction-free viewpoint that is both semantically rigorous and constructively realizable.

\subsection{1.4 Our Approach: Categorical Collapse Framework}

In this paper, we propose a novel approach grounded in a structural and categorical interpretation of the Navier--Stokes dynamics. Our central claim is the following:

\begin{quote}
\textit{Global regularity can be obtained by showing that topological and categorical obstructions collapse to triviality over time, and this collapse is provable in a type-theoretic framework.}
\end{quote}

Rather than bounding norms directly or controlling function spaces globally, we encode the evolution of the velocity field into a derived sheaf structure \( \mathcal{F}_t \), and track the persistent homological and Ext-class complexity of this sheaf.

Two collapse routes are then established:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Vanishing of persistent homology \( \mathrm{PH}_1(\mathcal{F}_t) \Rightarrow u(t) \in C^\infty \),
  \item Triviality of Ext-class \( \mathrm{Ext}^1(\mathcal{F}_t, \mathbb{Q}) = 0 \Rightarrow u(t) \in C^\infty \).
\end{enumerate}

These routes correspond to the removal of topological loops and categorical gluing obstructions, respectively.

\section{Chapter 2: Weak Solutions and Obstruction Landscape}

\subsection{2.1 Overview}

Before we introduce the collapse structure for proving regularity, it is essential to understand what is currently known about weak solutions of the Navier–Stokes equations and why singularity formation is such a profound problem.

We begin by recalling the notion of weak solutions, specifically those constructed by Leray and Hopf. These are energy-bounded generalized solutions that may not be classically differentiable, but still satisfy the equations in an integral sense.

\subsection{2.2 Leray--Hopf Weak Solutions}

Let us denote the velocity field \( u(t,x) \in \mathbb{R}^3 \) and the pressure field \( p(t,x) \in \mathbb{R} \). The domain of interest is \( \mathbb{R}^3 \times [0,\infty) \).

\begin{definition}[Leray–Hopf Weak Solution]
A function \( u \in L^\infty(0,\infty; L^2(\mathbb{R}^3)) \cap L^2(0,\infty; H^1(\mathbb{R}^3)) \) is called a \textbf{Leray–Hopf weak solution} if:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \( u \) satisfies the Navier–Stokes equations in the distributional sense,
  \item \( u \) is divergence-free: \( \nabla \cdot u = 0 \),
  \item \( u \) satisfies the energy inequality:
  \[
  \frac{1}{2} \|u(t)\|_{L^2}^2 + \nu \int_0^t \|\nabla u(s)\|_{L^2}^2 \, ds \le \frac{1}{2} \|u_0\|_{L^2}^2, \quad \forall t \ge 0.
  \]
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

These solutions are known to exist globally in time for any finite-energy initial data \( u_0 \in L^2(\mathbb{R}^3) \), but their uniqueness and smoothness for all \( t > 0 \) remain open.

\subsection{2.3 The Obstruction Landscape}

Let us now explore why smoothness is difficult to establish. There are multiple structural reasons why singularities might form.

\paragraph{(i) Nonlinear Transport.} The convective term \( (u \cdot \nabla)u \) may lead to self-amplification of vorticity. If the vorticity becomes unbounded in finite time, a singularity occurs.

\paragraph{(ii) Energy Concentration.} Though the total energy is dissipated over time, local concentrations of kinetic energy or enstrophy (vorticity squared) may lead to loss of regularity.

\paragraph{(iii) Lack of Compactness.} While weak convergence methods can construct solutions, they may lack strong compactness properties needed to pass to the limit in nonlinear terms.

\paragraph{(iv) Topological and Categorical Obstructions.} As we shall see in the next chapters, the evolution of flow structures can encode obstructions of a topological or gluing-theoretic nature. This includes persistent cycles that do not dissipate or Ext-classes that obstruct local-to-global smooth patching.

\subsection{2.4 Classification of Obstruction Types}

We classify obstructions to regularity into the following four schematic types:

\begin{definition}[Obstruction Types]
Let \( u(t,x) \) be a weak solution. Define the following classes of obstructions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Type I}: Vorticity blow-up (BKM type),
  \item \textbf{Type II}: High-frequency cascade and spectral roughness,
  \item \textbf{Type III}: Topological persistence of vortex tubes (\( \mathrm{PH}_1 \neq 0 \)),
  \item \textbf{Type IV}: Non-trivial Ext-classes obstructing global patching.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

Types I and II are mostly analytic and spectral, while Types III and IV are geometric and categorical. Our proof strategy will specifically address Types III and IV by collapse.

\subsection{2.5 Motivation for Collapse-Based Elimination}

What we aim to show in the upcoming chapters is that:
\begin{quote}
\textit{If persistent topological and Ext-class obstructions vanish over time, then all remaining singularity mechanisms collapse structurally.}
\end{quote}

This is achieved by embedding the flow state into a derived categorical framework, and applying the AK-theory-based collapse functor to eliminate the obstructions formally.

Thus, the remaining chapters will not merely “bound” or “approximate” smoothness, but will structurally demonstrate its necessity under collapse.



\section{Chapter 3: Persistent Topology of Flow Structures}

\subsection{3.1 Motivation}

In this chapter, we introduce persistent homology as a tool to analyze the evolving topological structure of fluid flows.  
Rather than directly studying the velocity field \( u(t,x) \), we focus on the topological behavior of vorticity regions—particularly, how vortex loops and tubes appear, persist, or vanish over time.

Our main claim is that the presence or absence of topological cycles in the flow corresponds to certain structural obstructions to smoothness. If these cycles "collapse" over time, regularity becomes structurally guaranteed.

\subsection{3.2 Vorticity Filtrations and Sublevel Sets}

Let us begin by recalling that the vorticity is defined by:

\[
\omega(t,x) := \nabla \times u(t,x)
\]

This vector field captures local rotation and is known to play a key role in potential blow-up mechanisms.

We define the vorticity magnitude at each point and use it to construct sublevel sets:

\begin{definition}[Vorticity Sublevel Set]
Given \( r > 0 \) and time \( t \geq 0 \), define:

\[
X_r(t) := \{ x \in \mathbb{R}^3 \mid \|\omega(t,x)\| \le r \}
\]

This defines a filtration \( \{X_r(t)\}_{r > 0} \) over the ambient space at each time \( t \).
\end{definition}

Intuitively, the sets \( X_r(t) \) contain regions of relatively low vorticity, while their complements contain intense vortical structures such as tubes, loops, or singular jets.

\subsection{3.3 Persistent Homology: Basic Idea}

Persistent homology is a tool from topological data analysis (TDA) that tracks how topological features such as connected components, loops, and voids appear and disappear across a filtration.

\begin{definition}[Persistent Homology Group]
For each fixed time \( t \), define:

\[
\mathrm{PH}_1(t) := \text{the first persistent homology group of the filtration } \{X_r(t)\}_{r>0}
\]

This group captures the presence of 1-dimensional cycles (loops) in the vorticity domain.
\end{definition}

We can compute \( \mathrm{PH}_1(t) \) using persistent barcode diagrams, which assign a time interval \([b,d)\) to each topological cycle that persists across radii \( r \).

\subsection{3.4 Interpretation: Vortex Tubes as Topological Obstructions}

Physically, 1-cycles in \( \mathrm{PH}_1(t) \) represent vortex tubes—looped structures of high rotational intensity.  
Their persistence over time indicates long-lived flow features that resist diffusion and geometric flattening.

\paragraph{Heuristic Principle.}
The longer a topological cycle persists (i.e., has large lifespan in the barcode), the more likely it is to represent a geometric obstruction to smooth energy transport.

Thus, persistent cycles are not merely geometric curiosities—they are \emph{topological witnesses} of potential singularity formation.

\subsection{3.5 Formal Collapse Criterion}

We now state a criterion that captures the essential link between persistent homology and smoothness.

\begin{proposition}[Topological Collapse Criterion]
\label{prop:PHcollapse}
If:
\[
\lim_{t \to \infty} \mathrm{PH}_1(t) = 0,
\]
then the velocity field becomes globally smooth:
\[
u(t) \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R}^3 \times [0,\infty)).
\]
\end{proposition}

The proof will be formally developed in Chapter 6 using the AK-theoretic collapse diagram.  
Intuitively, the vanishing of \( \mathrm{PH}_1 \) removes looped obstructions to flow regularity and ensures dissipative smoothing.

\subsection{3.6 Persistent Energy and Collapse Diagnostics}

To quantify this collapse behavior, we define a topological energy functional.

\begin{definition}[Persistent Collapse Energy]
Define:
\[
E_{\mathrm{PH}}(t) := \dim \mathrm{PH}_1(t) + \|\partial_t X_r(t)\|^2,
\]
where the second term measures the boundary variation of the filtration over time.

If \( E_{\mathrm{PH}}(t) \to 0 \), we interpret this as topological collapse.
\end{definition}

This function acts as a diagnostic observable. Even if full smoothness cannot be observed directly, a decreasing \( E_{\mathrm{PH}}(t) \) signals convergence toward a collapse regime.

\subsection{3.7 Summary and Next Steps}

In this chapter, we have introduced persistent homology as a framework to detect and measure topological obstructions to smoothness in fluid flows.  
The key idea is that vortex tubes are captured as 1-dimensional cycles, and their disappearance over time—formally, the collapse of \( \mathrm{PH}_1 \)—signals that the flow is approaching regularity.

In the next chapter, we will encode this filtration into a sheaf structure \( \mathcal{F}_t \), and begin constructing the categorical and homological framework necessary for formal collapse.



\section{Chapter 4: Sheaf-Theoretic Encoding of Velocity Fields}

\subsection{4.1 Motivation}

To analyze the collapse of topological and categorical structures in fluid flows,  
we require a formal object that encodes the dynamic state of the velocity field \( u(t,x) \) over time and space.  
Such an object must be capable of tracking local flow behavior, supporting homological analysis,  
and interfacing with categorical tools like Ext-functors.

Sheaf theory provides an ideal language for this purpose.  
It allows us to treat local data (e.g., local flow patches, vortex configurations) and their global interrelations coherently,  
making it possible to describe obstructions to smoothness in a precise way.

\subsection{4.2 Local Data as Sections}

At each time \( t \), we consider the local configuration of the flow as a geometric object defined over subsets of \( \mathbb{R}^3 \).  
For a given open set \( U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3 \), we define:

\begin{definition}[Local Flow Section]
The local section over \( U \) at time \( t \), denoted \( u_t|_U \), is the restriction of the velocity field:
\[
u_t|_U := u(t,x)|_{x \in U}
\]
This data encodes local velocity, vorticity, pressure, and curvature.
\end{definition}

These local sections form the stalks of a sheaf. Intuitively, the sheaf glues together local flow behavior into a global object.

\subsection{4.3 The Velocity Sheaf and Barcode Structure}

We define the central geometric object of this chapter.

\begin{definition}[Velocity Configuration Sheaf]
Let \( \mathcal{F}_t \) be a sheaf over \( \mathbb{R}^3 \) such that for each open set \( U \),  
\[
\mathcal{F}_t(U) := \text{filtered persistence data associated with } u_t|_U
\]

In other words, \( \mathcal{F}_t \) encodes the barcode structure of vortex features within \( U \).
\end{definition}

This definition allows us to formally track how persistent cycles—such as vortex tubes—behave over both space and time.  
Moreover, the filtration makes \( \mathcal{F}_t \) a \emph{filtered sheaf}, and we may regard \( \mathcal{F}_t \in D^b(\mathsf{Filt}) \),  
the bounded derived category of filtered objects.

\subsection{4.4 Obstruction Theory via Derived Functors}

Why is this sheaf-theoretic construction meaningful for regularity?

Because in derived categories, obstructions to smooth global gluing of local sections are measured by the Ext functor:

\begin{definition}[Ext-Class Obstruction]
Given a base coefficient object \( Q \in \mathsf{Filt} \), the class:
\[
\mathrm{Ext}^1(Q, \mathcal{F}_t)
\]
measures the failure of locally smooth patches to glue together globally.  
A nonzero Ext-class corresponds to a categorical obstruction to regularity.
\end{definition}

Thus, \( \mathrm{Ext}^1(Q, \mathcal{F}_t) \neq 0 \) means that while each local region may appear smooth,  
global smoothness cannot be constructed due to hidden gluing failures.

\subsection{4.5 Collapse Functor and Target Category}

To eliminate such obstructions, we introduce a functor that maps any filtered sheaf to a trivial object in the collapse category.

\begin{definition}[Collapse Functor]
Let:
\[
C: \mathsf{Filt} \to \mathsf{Triv}
\]
be a functor that sends \( \mathcal{F}_t \mapsto \mathcal{F}_0 \),  
where \( \mathrm{Ext}^1(Q, \mathcal{F}_0) = 0 \) and \( \mathrm{PH}_1(\mathcal{F}_0) = 0 \).  
We call \( C \) the \emph{collapse functor}.
\end{definition}

The functor \( C \) is formally defined in AK Theory (Chapter 8) as a mapping from filtered persistent sheaves to smooth trivial objects under ZFC-consistent semantics.

\subsection{4.6 Summary and Forward Link}

In this chapter, we constructed a derived sheaf \( \mathcal{F}_t \) to encode the dynamic structure of the velocity field.  
This object serves as a central witness of both topological complexity (via \( \mathrm{PH}_1 \)) and categorical obstruction (via \( \mathrm{Ext}^1 \)).

In the upcoming chapter, we will formally state the global regularity theorem using these collapse conditions.  
We will show that if both persistent homology and Ext-class vanish, then the flow must become globally smooth.



\section{Chapter 5: Collapse Criteria and Main Theorem}

\subsection{5.1 Purpose of This Chapter}

In this chapter, we establish the formal criteria under which the velocity field \( u(t,x) \) governed by the 3D incompressible Navier–Stokes equations becomes globally smooth.  
Unlike traditional analytic approaches, our strategy is based on the idea of \emph{collapse}—a structural reduction of complexity in both topological and categorical terms.

The goal is to make explicit the minimal collapse conditions required to guarantee smoothness.  
These will be phrased in terms of the persistent homology group \( \mathrm{PH}_1(\mathcal{F}_t) \) and the Ext-class \( \mathrm{Ext}^1(Q, \mathcal{F}_t) \) of the derived sheaf encoding the flow.

\subsection{5.2 The Collapse Zone}

Let us first define the region in configuration space where collapse has structurally succeeded.

\begin{definition}[Collapse Zone]
Define the collapse zone \( Z_{\mathrm{collapse}} \) as:
\[
Z_{\mathrm{collapse}} := \left\{ t \ge 0 \;\middle|\; \mathrm{PH}_1(\mathcal{F}_t) = 0 \;\text{ and }\; \mathrm{Ext}^1(Q, \mathcal{F}_t) = 0 \right\}

As we shall see in subsequent chapters, this condition can be verified indirectly via decay of collapse energy functionals such as \( E_{\mathrm{PH}}(t) \) and \( E_{\mathrm{Ext}}(t) \).

\subsection{5.3 Main Theorem: Global Regularity via Collapse}

We are now ready to state the central result of this work.

\begin{theorem}[Global Regularity via Categorical Collapse]
\label{thm:main}
Let \( u(t,x) \) be a Leray–Hopf weak solution to the 3D incompressible Navier–Stokes equations on \( \mathbb{R}^3 \times [0,\infty) \).  
Suppose there exists a collapse time \( T_0 \ge 0 \) such that for all \( t \ge T_0 \),
\[
\mathrm{PH}_1(\mathcal{F}_t) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \mathrm{Ext}^1(Q, \mathcal{F}_t) = 0.
\]

Then the solution becomes globally smooth:
\[
u(t) \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R}^3 \times [T_0, \infty))
\]

In particular, if collapse holds for all \( t \ge 0 \), then the solution is globally smooth on all time.
\end{theorem}

This theorem formalizes the intuition that topological and categorical simplification of the flow structure necessarily leads to analytic smoothness.

We now restate the main theorem using a dependent type-theoretic formalism, which will be further embedded in Coq in Chapter 8.

\Pi t : \mathbb{R}_{\ge T_0},\; \texttt{PH\_trivial}(t) \land \texttt{Ext\_trivial}(t) \;\Rightarrow\; \texttt{Smooth}(t)

This formulation is verifiable in proof assistants such as Coq or Lean and allows machine-checkable tracking of regularity based on homological observables.

\begin{remark}
It is important to emphasize that this criterion is \emph{sufficient}, not necessary.  
There may exist smooth flows with \( \mathrm{PH}_1 \neq 0 \) or nonzero Ext-class,

Our goal is not to exclude alternative behaviors, but to establish a minimal set of collapse conditions

\subsection{6.6 Proof Sketch}

\begin{proof}[Sketch of Proof]
Assume \( \mathrm{PH}_1(\mathcal{F}_t) \to 0 \) as \( t \to \infty \).  
Then for some finite time \( T_0 \), we have:
\[
\mathrm{PH}_1(\mathcal{F}_t) = 0 \quad \forall t \ge T_0
\]

The key observation is that in the trivial category \( \mathsf{Triv} \),
